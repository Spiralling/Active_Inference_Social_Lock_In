# Albarracin-like careful reader — cold read of "Changing a Networked Mind"

## The scene

Sunday morning, second coffee still hot, rain hitting the window of my apartment in Montreal. I have two hours blocked for IWAI reviews and this is the first one. The title made me sit up immediately — paradigm dynamics, networked agents, structure learning — that is squarely in my territory. I want this paper to be good. I want it the way you want a paper on your exact problem that you haven't had time to write yourself. My cat is on the chair beside me and I am entirely undistracted. I read every line.

## Reading it (stream of thought)

**Abstract.** First pass, already alert. "Theorems of the regime, not accidents of parameters" — bold claim. The abstract is doing a lot: homogeneity effect, reliability-as-latent, represented rivals. I am noting that [15] is cited here, which is my work with Hyland, and [1] is our epistemic communities paper. Let me see if they are actually using those or just flagging them. The abstract is unusually dense for IWAI — this feels like something that was probably a longer journal submission compressed to 18 pages. The 18 pages registers immediately. IWAI target is 10-12. Not a dealbreaker; Springer CCIS accepts up to 20 for strong submissions. But I am making a note.

**Page 2, Introduction.** The framing around open-endedness and machine-mediated science pipelines is smart and current — this puts the paper in conversation with ICML 2024 [14] and the broader AGI community without being sycophantic about it. The claim that "network epistemology cannot represent this case" is well-grounded: they are right that scalar credences on a fixed trust graph cannot carry structural revision. The phlogiston example is the right example — it is the canonical case precisely because the revision was not about a number, it was about what counted as a commitment at all. I am marking this paragraph to come back to: "a community that revised only its credences, leaving the network fixed, could never change frame at all." That sentence is doing load-bearing work and I want to see whether the formalism actually earns it.

Reference [1] appears at the bottom of page 2 in a cluster: "active-inference accounts supply precision-based lock-in [1], warnings that naive aggregation degrades collective precision [47, 48], and a single-agent variational cost of mind-change [15]." Fine. That is a fair characterization of our 2022 paper: precision-based lock-in is indeed what the epistemic communities model produces. But I notice the citation is in passing — they are citing what the prior work diagnoses, not engaging with how we modeled it. Which is acceptable if they are building something genuinely new, but I am watching for whether they recapitulate our structure without credit or depart from it without acknowledgment. [2], Catal et al.'s belief-sharing paper (in which I am a co-author), appears nowhere in the bibliography. I am looking again. No — it is reference [2] in the bibliography: "O. Catal, T. Van de Maele, R. J. Pitliya, M. Albarracin, C. Pattisapu, and T. Verbelen. Belief sharing: a blessing or a curse. arXiv:2407.02465, 2024." It is cited on page 10 in the results section. Good. Let me read that citation in context when I get there.

**Page 3, bottom — "scalar credence can never say how revising one commitment forces a re-reading of the rest."** Yes. This is the exact lacuna. And I am feeling something close to pleasure because this is the gap our epistemic communities paper gestures at but never formalizes: the structural propagation of revision. They are going to formalize it. Good.

**Section 2 — What a model must contain.** Three requirements: structure as object of inference, value, communication. The scaffolding is clean. The move to cite [15] for the value term — "value because scientists care about their conclusions" and citing Hyland and Albarracin — is correct. Our paper does derive exactly the variational cost of caring, the trade between accuracy and coherence with prior belief, weighted by how much that belief is valued. They are using our result as a building block rather than rediscovering it. That is the right relationship. What I want to check is whether Eq. (3) is actually our formulation or a variation. When I get to §3.2 I will look hard.

**Section 3.1.** The paradigm network G = (V, E, II) with nodes as theoretical commitments and edges as inferential dependencies — this is original and well-posed. The stochastic block model for the population is the right choice: it lets them sweep inter-community coupling cleanly. Figure 1 is helpful; I can see the architecture. The "belt" vs "core" distinction for sensory versus insulated commitments is nice — it has the flavor of Lakatos's protective belt but derived rather than stipulated. That derivation claim matters: they say conservatism (Eq. 1) is "recognized rather than posited." Let me check. Eq. (1): kappa_i = 1 + sum over descendants of pi_{i->w}. This is the Katz-Bonacich broadcast centrality applied to the agent's own network. Yes: the mass a revision would disturb is exactly this operator applied to a unit source. That is genuinely derived from the graphical structure, not posited. Good. I am less nervous now about the "theorem" claim.

**Section 3.2 — Motivated revision and the lock-in parameter.** Eq. (3): the variational free energy with the value term lambda * E_q[U^T x]. This is our formulation from [15] — specifically the tilted posterior q_lambda(x) proportional to p(x|o,G) * exp(lambda * U^T * x). Yes. They are citing us correctly and extending it to the network setting. The conviction field U propagated through the same operator T as conservatism — this is their contribution, not ours. The idea that conviction is the total effect of wanting things true through the paradigm's own causal structure is genuinely new and elegant. Eq. (2) follows from Eq. (1) with u_i attached to nodes — the same operator run on a different source vector. The fact that T*1 and T*u are linearly independent unless u is proportional to 1 means conservatism and conviction can decorrelate — a commitment can be load-bearing without being valued and vice versa. I am writing in the margin: "this is a genuinely new result on top of [15]. Not derivative. Good."

**Gamma (Eq. 4) — lock-in parameter.** The fraction of disconfirming sensory precision silenced by the core. This is a clean quantity: it measures how much the conviction-gated insulation actually blocks. I want to see what role it plays in the theorems. The paper says it is the boundary condition for the results in §4.

**Section 3.3 — Structure learning.** The reduction/expansion asymmetry as the formal counterpart of normal versus revolutionary science — this is the central conceptual move of the paper and it is well-executed. Reduction via Savage-Dickey (Eq. 5) closed-form on the already-inverted posterior — yes, that works in the linear-Gaussian regime. Expansion via Friston et al. [10] with Poisson-rate arrivals — also sound. The cost asymmetry (expansion requires real inversion, reduction does not) is a genuine structural result, not decoration. This is where the FEP vocabulary is actually load-bearing rather than cosmetic — the model comparison is variational inference, not just described as such.

**Section 3.4 — Forgetting.** Precision relaxation with omega as the forgetting factor — clean, inherited from [36-38]. The saturation result (Eq. 8 referenced but not shown) and the threshold conviction claim on page 8 are doing important work: conviction holds above a threshold so it becomes genuinely earned rather than structurally guaranteed. I would like to see Eq. 8 but it is in the appendix. I note that the appendix is "referenced but not in this PDF" — meaning the IWAI submission is not complete as reviewed. I am making a note. For a workshop this is acceptable, but I am somewhat annoyed that the derivations of the saturation threshold, the Savage-Dickey reduction, and the discovery rate (Appendix D) are all load-bearing and all missing.

**Section 3.5 — Reliability as a latent.** The Student-t gate via scale mixture, eta_k = (nu+1)/(nu+z_k^2), is classical [4,5,6] and they say so directly — citing O'Hagan [5,6] as the conflict-resolution literature that "has long owned the repair." This is honest scholarship. They are not claiming to have invented robust Bayes; they are applying it to a new setting and asking what it buys in the multi-agent case. I respect that framing. The key claim — "trust what does not keep surprising you" as a one-line characterization of the Student-t weight — is elegant. It captures the information-theoretic content exactly: channels that are on-prediction keep full weight; gross outliers are discounted as nu/z^2.

**Section 3.6 — Represented rivals.** Now this is where I sit forward. Each agent carries K candidate wirings and a posterior q_i(x,m) — not just q_i(x). Communication sends hypothesis weights (log-linear pooling of L_ik) between frames rather than averaging across frames. This is the key departure. Within a frame, beliefs fuse normally; between frames, what travels is the hypothesis itself as a structural object. This is the right architecture for incommensurability: two communities that hold structurally different hypotheses need to communicate the hypothesis, not the beliefs under it. The damped log-linear pooling L <- (1-alpha_m)*L + alpha_m*WL with the fixed-point analysis (~s/2*alpha_m nats gap) — I want to check this math. The claim is that the gate of §3.5 leaves consensus as the only fixed point for opinions but a genuine fixed gap for hypotheses when both camps have supporting evidence. If that is right, it is a real result. The paper says the proof is in Appendix C. Again, missing.

I am now holding two anxieties: (1) the appendices that carry all the proofs are absent, so the "theorems" I am being asked to evaluate are technically unverified; (2) the represented-rivals mechanism is given to the agents, not discovered — the K wirings are supplied, as they acknowledge on page 13. That is a significant limitation that they name honestly. But it means the mechanism demonstrates the geometry, not a full account of how paradigm alternatives arise. That is fine for a workshop paper; it would need to be named more prominently for a journal.

**Section 3.7 — The simulation at a glance.** The six-step loop is clear and internally consistent. Crisis and discovery as threshold crossings of the model's own evidence — genuinely derived from the model, not stipulated. The phlogiston instantiation (hub network, gravimetric anomaly at t_shift, oxygen not in any agent's network) is the right test case: small enough to be exact, representationally faithful to the historical episode.

**Section 4.1 — Homogeneity effect.** Figure 2 is the key result figure. Blue (disconnected): structural distance grows and holds. Red (connected, default fusion): structural distance stays at ~10% of disconnected. Green (wiring-gated trust): 94% of disconnected divergence survives. The result is clean and the comparison is fair — same world, same seeds, three architectures. The algebraic face of the homogeneity effect (|mean_i Pi_i| <= max_i |Pi_i| entrywise) is a real inequality. I am verifying mentally: entrywise, the average of matrices cannot have an entry larger than the largest entry any individual holds. Yes. That follows from convexity of the maximum and the fact that the fusion weights sum to one. This is provable. Good. What they cannot show analytically is the rate at which the collapse happens, which is why the simulation matters. The claim "conviction does not protect" — this is directly consonant with our [15] result that the variational cost of mind-change shifts where beliefs settle, not whether they yield. They are citing [15] for this correctly on page 11.

**Citation of [2] — Catal et al.** I find it on page 10: "A persistent school of thought would require an agent to weigh a message by its fit to the agent's own structure, and no quantity in the loop of §3.7 reads that fit; the hazard is recognized within active inference itself [2]." Correct: our belief-sharing paper identifies exactly this — that sharing under a common generative model degrades individual confidence in a way that is invisible to the sharing protocol. They are using [2] as a diagnostic reference, not a solution reference. That is an honest positioning: they are saying here is the problem our community already identified; here is what we build on top of it.

**Section 4.2 — Distinctness returns.** Figure 3 (single-agent variance under conflict, gated vs. baseline) is the most elegant result in the paper. The interior maximum — variance rises while evidence accumulates, then resolves — is exactly what "crisis as suspended confidence" should look like in a posterior. The Gaussian baseline showing no such maximum (monotonically falling variance, Theorem 2 made visible) is the right control. The gated agent reaching eta = 0.16, the channel flagged as unreliable, and the uncertainty peak at step 40 — that is a genuine phenomenological result. I am writing: "this is the figure I will cite in my review."

Figure 4 (inter-camp distance, log-log, t=1500 horizon) shows the three architectures' long-run behavior clearly. The represented rivals holding a flat gap at the fixed point while Student-t decays — the non-eroding tail slope claim in Appendix C is again load-bearing and missing. I believe it is true from the fixed-point analysis they sketch, but I cannot verify.

**Section 5 — Discussion.** The "value as precision" and "trust as inferred precision" framings are good — they properly situate the Kuhn claims not as allegory but as formal properties of the model. The incommensurability-as-state move (page 15) is the best theoretical contribution: incommensurability is not a failure of convergence but a fixed point of the damped hypothesis-pooling dynamics, a quantity two histories' accumulated confidence can reach. That deserves more emphasis.

The open problem statement about structure learning over the sensorium (page 15-16) is appropriately humble — they cannot place a prior over the unconceived, citing Stanford [27], they can only set the rate. That is honest and the limitation is real.

The machine-learning implication (page 15) — "a population of structure learners is only as open-ended as its pooling permits" — is the cleanest policy statement in the paper and the one that will land with the IWAI audience. I would move this to the abstract.

**Length.** 16 pages of main text plus 2 pages of bibliography. IWAI expects 10-12. They are 4-6 pages over. The model section (§3) alone is 6 pages of dense formalism. For a workshop this is a problem. The question is whether the additional length is justified by the content — and honestly, yes, the formalism is doing work throughout. But I would push them to cut: the forgetting section (§3.4, 0.5 pages) could be one paragraph; the simulation-at-a-glance (§3.7, 1 page) is useful but could be compressed to a table. They could probably get to 14 pages without losing anything load-bearing.

## After (the collapse)

- **How far I actually read:** All 16 pages of main text, all bibliography entries, twice through §3 (pages 5-9) and both result figures. I did not have access to appendices — they are referenced throughout but not included.

- **What I think they want from me / my takeaway:** The thesis, as I would restate it: under the field's own default choices — Gaussian belief propagation, precision-weighted pooling, linear-Gaussian structure learning — a community of active inference agents provably cannot sustain a paradigm conflict; conflict resolves into confident compromise. This is a null result about the natural baseline. The constructive content is that two specific departures (reliability as latent, structural hypothesis representation) restore what the defaults preclude: doubt as a posterior state, and structural pluralism as a fixed point rather than a transient. My reviewer verdict: **weak accept leaning accept**. The core contribution is real, the formalism is internally consistent within what I can check, the Kuhn framing is not allegory but is earned by the model's own threshold crossings, and the honest acknowledgment of boundaries is refreshing. What holds me back from a straight accept: the appendices are absent so the "theorems" are unverifiable; the paper is meaningfully over the page target; and the represented-rivals mechanism is given rather than discovered, which limits the revolution account.

- **Did I believe it? What lost me:** I believed the homogeneity effect and the variance-inflation result — those I can follow analytically. I believe the fixed-point argument for the wall because the log-linear pooling math is standard. What I cannot verify is the non-eroding tail slope and the saturation threshold derivation, both in missing appendices. The biggest conceptual gap is that the paper does not explain how an agent comes to hold K candidate wirings in the first place. The mechanism requires the rival to already be represented; it cannot account for how the rival was conceived. They acknowledge this (page 13-14) but I want it named earlier and more prominently — it is a founding assumption of the representational layer, not a limitation of the current implementation.

- **What I'd actually do next:** Write the review tonight. Score: 6 (weak accept). Demand in revisions: (1) appendices included or the "theorem" label softened to "proposition" with a sketch; (2) the K-wirings-are-given assumption surfaced by page 9 at the latest, not page 13; (3) explicit engagement with what the represented-rivals mechanism implies about the social process of conceiving rivals — the paper ends just where our epistemic communities work begins, and they should say so; (4) cut to ~14 pages. Strong encouragement to resubmit as a full journal article — this is not a workshop paper in ambition, and the IWAI community will benefit more from it as a paper they can cite at full length.

- **Would it land differently if I were fresh / less busy?:** The question inverts for me — a busier version of me would have missed the gap on the K-wirings assumption and probably scored it a 7 on the formalism alone. Full attention is what caught that the results section doesn't surface the limitation until page 13.
