# Albarracin-like careful reader — cold read of "Paradigm Shifts: Motivated Inference on Webs of Belief"

## The scene

Thursday evening, the quiet car out of Montreal, tray table open, noise-cancelling headphones on, train wifi dead. I promised myself one IWAI review tonight and I picked this one because the title is squarely my territory — motivated inference, webs of belief, paradigm shifts. I have been waiting for someone to try to formalize what our epistemic communities paper [1] gestured at but could not close: structural revision under social pressure. Two hours to my stop, nowhere to be, no distractions. I read every page including all four appendices, flipping back and forth to check claims against derivations.

## Reading it (stream of thought)

**Abstract.** Dense, confident, and immediately legible to me. "The field's default aggregation step — precision-weighted averaging — cannot reproduce the signature of paradigm change: it contracts the community onto a compromise network no member holds." This is a strong claim and I believe it is true — I have worried about exactly this in our federated inference papers [47, 48]. The abstract names all four mechanisms: the propagation operator, value tilt, inferred reliability, represented rivals. And it ends with the machine-learning implication — engineering communities whose aggregation protocols decide the question by design. That last line is the policy claim that will land with the IWAI audience and it is good to have it here.

I note immediately: the paper has been retitled since whatever earlier draft I vaguely associate with this project. "Motivated Inference on Webs of Belief" is a better title than whatever it was before — it names both the mechanism (motivated inference) and the object (webs, not just networks, which gets at the structure-not-just-credences point). The keywords include "belief sharing" — which is [2], my paper with Catal et al. Let me track how they use it.

**Page 1, §1 Introduction.** The "changing a frame, not a credence" framing is right. They cite Kuhn [19] honestly — "Kuhn named but could not formalise" — and they point at exactly what the formalisation must do: explain why communities hold commitments past disconfirming evidence and why, when they do give way, they give way fast. The phlogiston example on page 2 is the canonical case for exactly the right reason: the revision was not about any one number, it was about which entanglements to keep. The web metaphor is earned here, not decorative.

Second paragraph of page 2 is where they position relative to the literature. They name our work at [1] for "precision-based lock-in" — accurate — and [47, 48] for warnings about naive aggregation — accurate — and [15], Hyland & Albarracin, for the "single-agent variational cost of mind-change" — correct. I am checking the precise characterisation: they say [15] is a single-agent result, which is true; our paper does not model communication. So the social extension they are building is genuinely new territory. Good. They also name Thagard's ECHO [30, 41] as a predecessor that captures coherence but lacks the "directed dependencies and the carry-over cost of mind-change derived below." That is a fair summary of ECHO's limits. What ECHO does not have is a generative model with a prior precision structure — it has undirected coherence weights, not a Bayes net. So the departure is real.

Reference [2] — Catal et al., belief sharing — I am looking for it. It is in the bibliography. Where does it appear in the text? Page 2 of the introduction does not cite it. I will continue looking.

**Page 3, §2 Desiderata.** Three requirements: structure as the object of inference, value, communication. Clean. The move to cite [15] for the value desideratum is correct — we proved the variational cost of caring is a tilt on the posterior, not a modification of the prior. They then say the paper builds on "exactly these defaults, on purpose." That rhetorical move — building from the field's defaults so the failure mode is provably the default's failure, not a special case — is the right strategy. It means the null result is airtight.

**Page 3–5, §3.1 — The population and paradigm network.** G = (V, E, II), nodes as theoretical commitments, edges as inferential dependencies, coupling precisions pi_uv. Trust graph as stochastic block model with inter-community weight swept from isolation to dense contact. Figure 1 is clear.

The propagation operator T = (I - A)^{-1} as a Neumann series. This is Wright's path analysis [74] modernised — the path coefficients are the coupling precisions and T_{iw} is the total precision-weighted coupling from i to w along all directed paths. I am noting that this is finite on a DAG because A is nilpotent. Correct.

Eq. (1): kappa_i = T1, the conservatism field. This is the Katz broadcast centrality of the agent's own network, read as revision cost. The claim "conservatism is recognised rather than posited" is tested right here. And it holds: the revision cost is exactly the total downstream mass that moves when i moves — that is the centrality. It is derived from the graphical structure, not assigned by hand. I am satisfied.

Eq. (2): U = Tu, the conviction field. Same operator, different source vector. I am checking: the intrinsic utility u_i propagates through T along the same dependency paths as the conservatism. So the conviction field is the total effect of wanting things true through the paradigm's own causal structure. And the paper notes T1 and Tu are linearly independent unless u is proportional to 1. This means a commitment can be load-bearing (high kappa) without being valued (low U) and vice versa. This is a genuinely new decomposition relative to [15] — we did not model the network topology, so we could not separate these two fields. I am writing in the margin: "original contribution, well-derived."

**Page 5–6, §3.2 — Motivated revision.** Eq. (3): F[q] = D_KL[q(x)||p(x|G)] - E_q[log p(o|x,G)] - lambda * E_q[U^T x]. This is [15] extended to the network. The tilted posterior q_lambda proportional to p(x|o,G) * exp(lambda * U^T x) — yes, this is our result. They use it correctly. The KL is anchored to the *network prior* p(x|G) — which is the right choice; mind-change is priced from where the paradigm currently stands, not from some external reference. Appendix A (p.17) derives the exact posterior in closed form as a mean shift: q_lambda = N(mu + lambda*SigmaU, Sigma) with the value displacing the mean without changing the covariance. I flipped to check this and it is correct.

Eq. (4): the lock-in parameter gamma — the fraction of disconfirming sensory precision silenced by the core. The sum in the numerator is over belt channels that are gated by the core, weighted by rho_k. This is the per-channel conviction-gate weight w_k = exp(-g|U * H_k|) entered into the denominator suppression. I am following the algebra: as conviction drives a channel's precision toward zero, the channel can no longer pull the belief back. The fraction silenced is gamma. This is a derived quantity, not a tuned parameter — it falls out of the conviction geometry. Good.

**Page 6–7, §3.2 continued — structure learning.** The Savage-Dickey ratio (Eq. 5) for scoring candidate prunes. I flip to Appendix D.2 (p.26) to check the derivation. The evidence half is Bayesian model reduction [8, 11] — the Savage-Dickey identity scores the swap of prior (II_0, h_0) for the edge-e-reduced prior (II_0^(e), h_0^(e)) under the same likelihood, and the paper says it "already exhibits the reduced posterior." Eqs. (10) and (11) give the closed forms. Eq. (10): II^(e) = II_0 + J, h^(e) = h_0 + j, with J the likelihood deposit and j its natural-form mean. Eq. (11): DeltaU_e = U^T(mu^(e) - mu). This is one linear solve per candidate edge. I am satisfied — the reduction is genuinely closed-form and the value cost is correctly computed on the edge-e-reduced posterior mean. The text says "the value half costs one linear solve per candidate edge" — accurate.

The conviction-value change DeltaU < 0 for a pruning that moves the posterior mean away from cherished commitments. The rule says "evidence must overcome lambda times the value the edge protects." This is the Kuhnian core and it is derived, not stipulated. I am happy with this.

What the paper does NOT do is expansion — I note the explicit acknowledgment on page 7: "The model in this paper runs only reduction." And the honesty about why: expansion raises Stanford's problem of unconceived alternatives [27]. Poisson-rate arrivals are used in the earlier prototype (Appendix B) but not in the main model. This is a real scope limitation but it is named clearly and in the right place. Marking it as a limitation I will need to flag in my review score without penalising it as a flaw — it is an honest boundary.

**Page 7–8, §3.3 — Representing diversity.** The two mechanisms: weights on observations (inferred reliability, eta_k, Eq. 6) and weights over structures (represented rivals, K candidate wirings with posterior q(m)).

Eq. (6): eta_k = (nu+1)/(nu + z_k^2). The Student-t weight. This is classical Bayesian robust inference [5, 6], and they cite O'Hagan correctly. The characterisation "trust what does not keep surprising you" is clean. I flip to Appendix C (p.22) for the three reads — channels, opinions, wiring — and I find the formula for z_{ij}^2 on channel opinions: the calibrated disagreement of two posteriors summed over the measured nodes, normalized by the sum of their marginal variances. This is the right quantity: it reads structural disagreement, not disagreement in means, because it uses the full (II^{-1}[v,v] + II_j^{-1}[v,v]) denominator. I am checking: this means a localized heresy at one node can sever trust from a neighbour globally if their marginals disagree at that node. That is the right behavior for incommensurability.

The wiring read: z^2 taken over contested coupling entries x = II[a,c], normalized by their pairwise symmetric scale max((x_i^2 + x_j^2)/2, epsilon^2), bounded per coupling, invariant to global precision growth. I am reading this slowly. The normalization is designed to make the wiring gate read *how* two agents wire the world rather than how much evidence they hold. That caveat at the end of Appendix C is exactly right: "the data's verdict on an unsupported theory lives in the means, and in the strain and reliability reads that live on them." Scope stated precisely. Rare and welcome.

**Represented rivals (pp. 7–8).** K candidate wirings (II_k, h_k) with log-weights L_{ik}. Per-step: each candidate scored by its prequential predictive density *before* the shared deposit lands. Structure-discriminating evidence accumulates in log-weights L rather than being erased by the Fisher deposit. Fusion is hypothesis-aligned — candidate k pools only with neighbours' candidate k, never averaged across frames. Damped log-linear pooling: L <- (1-alpha_m)L + alpha_m*WL.

I am checking Appendix C for the fixed-point analysis. The gap at the fixed point converges to (I-W)^+(s - s-bar)/alpha_m, approximately s/2*alpha_m nats for two symmetric camps. The paper claims this is a genuine fixed point — "the wall of Fig. 3 — whose height the early-measured per-step source predicts conservatively." Fig. 3 on p.10 shows the flat gap at D(t) out to t=1500. I am looking at the number: 2.5*10^{-4} for plain fusion, 0.075 for gated, 2.79 for represented rivals, with q_own = 1.0 in both rival camps. The scale difference is two orders of magnitude between the gated and the represented architecture. That is a real result, not a marginal effect.

But I want to be honest: the fixed-point derivation in Appendix C gives me the form of the gap (proportional to the signal strength s and inversely to alpha_m) but does not prove that the wall is stable against perturbations of K — i.e., what happens if one camp discovers a new hypothesis that the other camp has not conceived. The paper acknowledges this on p.11: "under log-linear pooling a lone discoverer's candidate is majority-swamped." This is the discoverer problem, and it is real. They name it honestly in the discussion and limitations section.

**Page 8–9, §3.4 — Simulation at a glance.** The five-step loop. I am reading carefully because this is where earlier papers sometimes smuggle in assumptions via the implementation without flagging them. Here: step 3 (crisis/reduction) scores candidate prunes by DeltaF + lambda_i * DeltaU, both closed-form from the Savage-Dickey reduced posterior. Step 4 (fuse): posteriors and structural edits pool across trust graph, weighted by inter/intra coupling and by eta_ij with the social gate on. Step 5 (forget): precision decays by omega.

The note that the loop does NOT contain an expansion step is made explicitly and correctly. And the sentence "nothing exotic has been smuggled in" — I am holding this claim up to the derivations I have just checked in Appendices A and D, and I believe it. Every ingredient is a field default or a direct extension of one. The result that these defaults *together* cannot produce paradigm change is a genuine provable fact about the architecture, not a tuning choice.

**Page 9–11, §4 Results.** Two results, two figures.

Result 1 (Fig. 3, §4.1): plain fusion collapses in a few steps; Student-t gate buys time (decays); represented rivals hold flat to t=1500. The algebraic face: |mean_i II_i| <= max_i |II_i| entrywise. I verify this mentally — yes, the average of precision matrices cannot have an entry larger than any individual holds, because the averaging weights are non-negative and sum to one. This is a real inequality. The contraction is structural, not parameter-dependent. Good.

The sentence "whatever a collective knows beyond its members lives in the *union* of their networks, never in their average" — this is the clearest statement of the main theorem and it lands. It is also directly relevant to the design of AI reasoning communities, and I want to see it in the abstract or at minimum the introduction. It is currently buried mid-paragraph on page 9.

Result 2 (Fig. 4, §4.2): The Kuhn cycle — normal science, crisis, revolution, new normal — read off the model posterior q(oxygen) and the entropy H(q(m)). Normal science forms endogenously from uniform start. Crisis as a state of the belief: the entropy of q(m) peaks in the shaded band while q(oxygen) climbs. Then resolution. The fact that all four phases are visible in the model's own belief state, without being stipulated events, is the paper's best result. I am writing: "this is the figure. If I were writing a review blurb, this figure is why I would say accept."

But I have a complaint about Fig. 4. The x-axis runs to step 250 and the world turns at t=100 (dotted line). The crisis band is the shaded region approximately steps 120-170. But the y-axis on the right is "posterior entropy H(q(m))" and the scale tops at 1.0. For K=2 candidates, the maximum entropy is log(2) = 0.693 nats, not 1.0. Is the entropy normalized by log(K)? The paper does not say explicitly. If it is, fine. If it is not, the peak at ~0.6 on the right axis is close to log(2)/1.0 and consistent, but the axis label should say "H(q(m)) / log(K)" or note normalization. This is a small but concrete error that should be fixed.

**Page 11–12, §5 Discussion.** The homogenization-as-representation-failure framing is correct and important: "there is no coordinate in (II, h) whose value can mean *torn between two hypotheses*." This is the crisp statement of why the Gaussian baseline fails. Not a parameter choice, a representational limit. Good.

The relation to Kuhn is allegory-free now: the model *holds* normal science, *infers* crisis, *represents* incommensurability as a state. The move to cite Kuhn [65] on commensurability/comparability/communicability in the context of incommensurability-as-timescale is philosophically precise. I checked [65] — it is the 1983 PSA paper, not the 1962 book, which is the right citation for Kuhn's mature view of incommensurability as something less than total. The paper is citing Kuhn correctly, not just decoratively.

Limitations paragraph: "the demonstrations are small and the nets hand-wired; the claims are existence proofs about representation, not sweeps of social topologies." Honest and accurate. The K-wirings-are-given assumption is named here — "no model expansion runs anywhere in the main text." I had wanted this surfaced earlier (it is a founding assumption of the rival layer, not a limitation of the current run), and it appears here on p.11 rather than at the start of §3.3 where the represented rivals are introduced. That is still a little late. A single sentence in §3.3 — "we give agents their K candidates; how rivals arise is outside this scope" — would prevent a reader from spending half of §3.3 wondering.

**Appendix A** (pp. 17–18): the closed-form derivations. The SEM x = Ax + zeta, zeta ~ N(0, D^{-1}), giving T = (I-A)^{-1} as the resolvent. The value tilt as a pure mean shift: q_lambda = N(mu + lambda*SigmaU, Sigma). The KL cost (Eq. 8): D_KL[q_lambda || p(x|G)] = half*delta^T*II_0*delta + half*[tr(II_0*Sigma) - log det(II_0*Sigma) - d], with delta = mu + lambda*SigmaU - mu_0. I am checking this. The KL between two Gaussians with the same covariance is half*delta^T*II*delta — but here the KL is anchored to the *network prior* II_0, not the posterior II. The term tr(II_0*Sigma) - log det(II_0*Sigma) - d is the trace term from the different covariances. This is correct — the mind-change cost uses the prior precision because that is the metric from which the mind is changing. Good. The Appendix A note about the transpose convention ("a reader reproducing T1 literally against the code's array must transpose first") is a sign that this was debugged against code. The released code and the paper are consistent. Useful flag for reproducibility.

**Appendix B** (pp. 17–22): the null regime and the earlier prototype. The phlogiston world is described carefully — ten named commitments, the hub (phlogiston) as common cause of eight surface commitments, the gravimetric anomaly as the anomaly node whose prior mean is wrong (phlogiston escapes so calx should be lighter). The rival drops hub couplings and keeps the mass law with the correct means — exactly the Schur residue of marginalizing the hub, as Appendix D.3 then derives. The flagging of earlier-prototype numbers versus main-text numbers is careful and explicit. Table 1 lists which dials belong to which model. This is unusual transparency for appendix material and I appreciate it.

The result "only total isolation protects a superseded paradigm" (Fig. 6) — at inter=0.005, 92% convert even under strong gating. The sharp boundary, not a gradual fade — this is a real result. The control (conviction exactly as high, self-gating switched off, revolted fraction 1.0 vs. gamma=1) shows the gate is doing the stalling. I believe this.

The "pooling kills the lone discoverer" result (Fig. 7): under delta pooling, discovery probability is zero at every rate (survival tracks zero). Under abstention pooling, survival tracks the discovery fraction (0.39/0.64/0.86 at three rates). This is a striking result and it belongs in the main text, not the appendix. I understand why it is in the prototype appendix — the expansion mechanism is not in the main model — but the policy implication ("incommensurability as a population-level force is a property of the protocol, not of the concepts") is one of the paper's best lines and it is buried on p.21.

**Appendix C** (pp. 22–25): the exact definitions. The three reads of the Student-t gate, the represented-rivals architecture, the experiment details. This appendix earns its length. The closing paragraph on scope — the three commitments that bound the results — is admirably honest. Point (iii): "the wiring gate can protect a pluralism but cannot tell a supported theory from a merely committed one." This is a real limitation and it is good that they found it by running the model rather than deducing it from theory.

**Appendix D** (pp. 25–28): the crisis check, the Schur residue, and the discovery check. D.1-D.2: the crisis check derived from the single tilted objective. Eq. (9): score(M -> M-tilde) = DeltaF + DeltaG + lambda*DeltaU. DeltaF is the log Bayes factor. DeltaG is the expected information gain for any newly introduced latent. DeltaU is the conviction-field value change. The claim that this is Eq. (3) evaluated at two structures and differenced — yes, that is exactly right. The crisis check is not a new mechanism; it is the same objective scored comparatively.

D.2: the crisis check as a prune. Eq. (10) and (11) I already checked above. The comparison to the static surrogate (DeltaF_e > lambda*v_e) is useful: the static threshold uses the summed conviction magnitude at the edge's endpoints as a stand-in, but the paper correctly notes that lambda*DeltaU_e *shrinks as evidence accumulates* because the reduced and full posteriors share the likelihood deposit. A data-rich paradigm is barely protected at the threshold. Lock-in comes from the conviction gate that decides which evidence is allowed to accumulate at all (Eq. 4), not from a high bar at the moment of decision. This is the paper's most important theoretical insight and it is completely correct. I want to see it in the main text.

D.3: the Schur residue. The matrix computation (Eq. 12) is correct — marginalizing a hub from a common-cause structure over (x1, x2, b) produces the direct coupling -cc^T/II_{bb} in the visible precision, exactly the footprint the discovery check reads off. This is elegant and it earns the claim that the rival paradigm (oxygen) is the Schur complement of the incumbent's hub marginalization.

D.4: the discovery check (prototype only). D.5: what remains a modelling commitment. The belt conjunction and the ordering are named as commitments, not results. The Poisson rate r, the prediction-error window, the warm-up, and the hub's self-precision are quantitative choices. This is a rarity in active inference papers: explicit documentation of what is committed versus what is derived. I am almost moved. Almost.

**On the FEP credentials.** A recurring anxiety when I read papers in this space is: is this active inference, or is this Gaussian belief propagation wearing an FEP hat? Here: the bounded agents are minimizing variational free energy step by step (p.4, citing [39]), they are myopic (no planning horizon), and their structure learning uses Bayesian model reduction [8, 11] which is a genuine free-energy operation on model priors. The conviction field and the tilt posterior are derived from the free-energy objective, not attached to it post-hoc. The model is real active inference — bounded, myopic, generative-model-based — not Gaussian message passing with FEP vocabulary. That is a real distinction and this paper is on the right side of it.

**What is missing.** The activation of [2] and [46] — ghost references from my own lineage. The K-wirings-are-given assumption surfaced a page too late. The Fig. 4 entropy normalization ambiguity. And one thing nobody mentions enough: the paper never asks what happens to an agent that *disagrees with the community's paradigm shift* — our epistemic communities paper [1] shows that minority agents under precision-weighted lock-in can have their effective precision reduced to the point where they are functionally silenced. This paper shows that the *community* can hold a paradigm; it does not ask what happens to the deviant who held the old one after the flip. That is a real open question and one that this model is actually equipped to address — the conviction field U is per-agent, so one could track the fate of agents with heterogeneous u. They should say so in the discussion.
